From the Big Bang
to the First Life on Earth

Science•23 November 2026

How a tiny, hot dot grew into a whole universe, made stars, built our planet, and finally made life. Let’s travel through time!

▶ Watch the story: from the Big Bang to the first life on Earth (78 seconds)

Did you know? If the whole history of the universe were squeezed into one year, the Big Bang would be on 1 January, the Sun and Earth would form at the end of August, the first life would appear around 21 September, and humans would arrive at about 11:48 pm on 31 December!

1 The Big Bang

13.8 billion years ago

Long, long ago, everything in the universe was squeezed into a tiny, super-hot spot. Then space itself started to stretch, like a balloon being blown up. It was not an explosion in one place. It happened everywhere at once, and the universe is still stretching today!

Read it left to right: the universe starts tiny on the left and keeps getting bigger, with stars and galaxies appearing as it grows. (Picture: NASA / WMAP Science Team)

Time moves on!

2 The first atoms

3 minutes to 380,000 years after the Big Bang

At first the universe was a boiling soup of tiny bits. As it cooled, they joined up to make the two simplest things: hydrogen and helium. After 380,000 years it was cool enough for whole atoms to form, and light could finally travel. We can still see that very old light today!

The universe’s baby picture. These speckles are the oldest light we can see. They were the seeds of every galaxy. (Picture: Mark McCaughrean, CC BY-SA 4.0, based on ESA Planck data)

Time moves on!

3 The first stars light up

About 200 million years after the Big Bang

For millions of years the universe was dark, with only clouds of gas. Then gravity pulled the gas into big balls that got so hot they started to shine. These were the first stars, much bigger and brighter than our Sun!

An artist’s picture of the first stars switching on in the dark young universe. (Picture: NASA / WMAP Science Team)

A real star nursery. New stars are still being born inside these giant clouds of gas and dust today. (Picture: NASA, ESA, Hubble Heritage Team)

Time moves on!

4 Stars cook the ingredients of life

Over billions of years

Stars are like giant kitchens. Inside them, hydrogen and helium are squeezed into new things like carbon, oxygen and iron. When a big star dies, it explodes as a supernova and throws these new ingredients into space. New stars and planets are made from them. That is why we say we are made of stardust!

The Crab Nebula is what is left of a star that exploded. Its glowing gas is full of new ingredients. (Picture: NASA, ESA, J. Hester and A. Loll)

Time moves on!

5 Our Sun and Earth are born

4.6 billion years ago

A cloud of gas and stardust squeezed together. The middle became our Sun. The leftover dust spun around it in a flat disk and slowly stuck together into planets, including Earth.

A real baby solar system! This young star has a dusty disk around it. The dark rings may be where new planets are forming, just like Earth did. (Picture: ALMA (ESO/NAOJ/NRAO), CC BY 4.0)

Time moves on!

6 A giant crash makes the Moon

About 4.5 billion years ago

Baby Earth was hot and full of melted rock. Scientists think a planet about the size of Mars, called Theia, crashed into it. The pieces thrown into space came together and became our Moon.

An artist’s picture of two young planets crashing, a bit like the crash that made our Moon. (Picture: NASA / JPL-Caltech)

Our Moon today, made from that ancient crash. (Picture: Gregory H. Revera, CC BY-SA 3.0)

Time moves on!

7 Oceans fill the Earth

About 4.4 to 4 billion years ago

Slowly, Earth cooled down. Water came from volcanoes and from icy rocks from space, and filled the first oceans. Earth was getting ready for life!

Young Earth was hot, with lots of volcanoes, lava and space rocks. (Picture: Tim Bertelink, CC BY-SA 4.0)

Earth today, photographed by astronauts. Look at all that blue ocean! (Picture: NASA / Apollo 17 crew)

Time moves on!

8 The first life begins!

About 4 to 3.5 billion years ago

In the young oceans, simple chemicals, water and energy from volcanoes, lightning and sunlight mixed together. They made the building blocks of life. Some molecules learned to copy themselves, and got wrapped in tiny bubbles. These became the very first living cells, far too small to see!

A deep-sea hot vent. Many scientists think life may have started near warm, chemical-rich places like this. (Picture: P. Rona, NOAA)

A famous experiment (1952): scientists sparked fake lightning through gases and water, and made some building blocks of life in a jar! (Picture: YassineMrabet, CC BY-SA 3.0)

Stromatolites are rocks built by tiny living things. Fossils like these, 3.5 billion years old, are some of the oldest signs of life on Earth. (Picture: Paul Harrison, CC BY-SA 3.0)

And then...

9 What happened next

Tiny living things called cyanobacteria learned to use sunlight. They slowly filled the air with oxygen, about 2.4 billion years ago. Much later, about 540 million years ago, the sea filled with all kinds of animals. And just 300,000 years ago, humans appeared!

Cyanobacteria, the tiny oxygen makers. We can breathe today thanks to them! (Picture: NASA)

Early sea animals, about 500 million years ago. Some looked very strange! (Picture: PaleoEquii, CC BY-SA 4.0)

? Big mysteries still to solve

Scientists know a lot, but some questions are still open:

• What happened at the very first moment, and was there anything before it?
• Exactly how did non-living chemicals turn into the first living cell?
• Is there life on other planets?

Maybe you will be the scientist who solves one of these one day!
