How We Became
Human

Science•26 November 2026

Who were your great-great-great-grandparents? Not only people! Long ago they were fish, tiny furry animals and apes. This is the story of our own family, from one tiny cell all the way to modern humans like you and me.

Did you know? If a new grandmother were born every 25 years, your great-great-great-...-grandmother who lived 7 million years ago would be about 280,000 grandmothers back. And all of them were living in Africa!

1 One tiny cell

More than 3.5 billion years ago

Our family story starts with one tiny cell in the sea. It was so small that you could not see it. However, it could do something amazing: it could make copies of itself. Every plant, every animal and every person comes from this tiny beginning. Even you started your life as just one tiny cell!

Tiny cells under a microscope. The first living things were small and simple, a bit like these. (Picture: Iceclanl, CC BY-SA 3.0)

Time moves on!

2 We become fish

About 530 million years ago

After a very long time, some animals in the sea grew a stiff rod along their back. This was the start of a backbone. These little animals were the first fish. Touch the middle of your back. Can you feel the bumps? That backbone first came from these tiny fish!

Haikouichthys was one of the first fish. It was only as long as your finger. (Picture: Nobu Tamura, CC BY-SA 3.0)

Time moves on!

3 Fins become legs

About 375 million years ago

Some fish had strong fins with bones inside. They used them to push through shallow water and mud. Slowly, over millions of years, these fins became legs with fingers and toes. Look at your arm: one bone at the top, two bones in the middle, then small bones in your hand. These old fish had the same plan!

Tiktaalik was half fish and half land animal. It had gills, but it also had a neck and strong fins like little arms. (Picture: Zina Deretsky, National Science Foundation, public domain)

Time moves on!

4 The first mammals

About 300 to 205 million years ago

Some land animals laid eggs with a shell, so they did not need water any more. One branch of this family slowly grew hair and fed their babies with milk. They became the first mammals. They were tiny, like a mouse, and they came out at night. Why? Because the giant dinosaurs ruled the day!

Dimetrodon looks like a dinosaur, but it is not! It belongs to the old family that later gave us the mammals. (Picture: Nobu Tamura, CC BY-SA 4.0)

Morganucodon was one of the first mammals. It was about as big as a mouse. (Picture: FunkMonk, CC BY-SA 3.0)

Time moves on!

5 The first primates

About 66 to 55 million years ago

Then a giant space rock hit the Earth, and the big dinosaurs disappeared. Now the little mammals had a chance! Some of them climbed up into the trees. They got hands that could hold branches, flat nails instead of claws, and eyes at the front to judge jumps. They were the first primates, the family of monkeys, apes and us.

A lemur is a primate cousin who lives today. Look at its hands and the eyes at the front of its face! (Picture: Charles J. Sharp, CC BY-SA 4.0)

Time moves on!

6 Apes and our cousin, the chimp

About 25 to 7 million years ago

Some primates lost their tails. They became the apes. They were bigger and very clever. About 7 million years ago, one ape family split into two. One side became chimpanzees. The other side became us! So chimps are not our grandparents. They are our cousins. We still share about 99 out of every 100 letters of our DNA with them.

Proconsul was an early ape. It lived in Africa about 18 million years ago and had no tail, like us. (Picture: Ghedoghedo, CC BY-SA 4.0)

A chimpanzee in Uganda. Chimps use sticks as tools, just like our early family did. (Picture: Giles Laurent, CC BY-SA 4.0)

Time moves on!

7 Standing up on two legs

About 7 to 3 million years ago

In Africa, our early family started to walk on two legs. Maybe it helped them to see over tall grass, or to carry food in their hands. Their brains were still small, like a chimp. However, their legs and feet were changing, step by step.

Toumaï is about 7 million years old. It was found in Chad in 2001. It may be one of the oldest members of our family. (Picture: Didier Descouens, CC BY-SA 4.0)

Ardi's skull. Ardi lived about 4.4 million years ago in Ethiopia. She could walk on two legs and also climb trees. (Picture: Ghedoghedo, CC BY-SA 4.0)

The Laetoli footprints in Tanzania. About 3.7 million years ago, two of our relatives walked through wet volcanic ash. Their footprints are still there! (Picture: Momotarou2012, CC BY-SA 3.0)

Time moves on!

8 Tools, fire and travellers

About 3.3 to 1 million years ago

Our family learned to hit stones together to make sharp tools. The oldest stone tools are about 3.3 million years old. Then came Homo, the human family. Homo habilis means "handy man". Later, Homo erectus was tall, with long legs for walking far. They were the first in our family to leave Africa, and in time they learned to use fire.

Homo habilis, one of the first members of the human family. Its brain was bigger than a chimp's. (Picture: Rama, CC BY-SA 3.0 FR)

Turkana Boy was a young Homo erectus who lived in Kenya about 1.5 million years ago. He was already very tall! (Picture: Emőke Dénes, CC BY-SA 4.0)

A hand axe. Homo erectus and later humans made these clever teardrop tools for more than a million years. (Picture: The Portable Antiquities Scheme, CC BY-SA 2.0)

Time moves on!

9 Many kinds of humans

About 400,000 to 40,000 years ago

Not long ago, there were many kinds of humans on Earth at the same time! Neanderthals lived in Europe and West Asia. Denisovans lived in Asia. Tiny "hobbit" people, only about 1 metre tall, lived on an island in Indonesia. And in Africa, about 300,000 years ago, people like us appeared. Our name is Homo sapiens, which means "wise human".

A Neanderthal man, made by scientists from real bones. Neanderthals were strong, clever and cared for their sick. (Picture: Werner Ustorf, CC BY-SA 2.0)

The Harbin skull from China is at least 146,000 years old. In 2025, scientists found clues that it was a Denisovan. For the first time, we could see a Denisovan face! (Picture: Fu et al. 2025, CC BY 4.0)

The "hobbit" skull from the island of Flores, Indonesia. These little people had a brain about the size of a chimp's, but they still made stone tools. (Picture: Rama, CC BY-SA 3.0 FR)

The oldest Homo sapiens ever found! This skull, called Jebel Irhoud 1, was found in Morocco in 1961. It is about 300,000 years old. (Picture: Jonathan Chen, CC BY-SA 4.0)

Time moves on!

10 Our big journey around the world

About 60,000 to 15,000 years ago

About 50,000 to 60,000 years ago, small groups of our people walked out of Africa. Step by step, over many, many lifetimes, they reached Asia, Europe and even Australia by boat. Later, they walked into the Americas. Along the way, they painted animals and hands on cave walls and carved little statues. Slowly, all the other kinds of humans disappeared. In the end, we were the only humans left.

Out of Africa. The arrows show how our people walked and sailed from Africa to every part of the world. (Picture: Jayasinghe23, CC BY-SA 4.0)

Hand pictures in a cave in Indonesia. Some hand pictures in these caves are about 40,000 years old. It is like the people are saying, "We were here!" (Picture: Sabjan Badio, CC BY-SA 4.0)

Horses and other animals painted in Chauvet Cave, France, more than 30,000 years ago. This is a copy in a museum. (Picture: HTO, public domain)

Footprints at White Sands, USA. They are about 21,000 to 23,000 years old. They show that people reached America very early. (Picture: U.S. Geological Survey, public domain)

A family secret! When our people left Africa, they met the Neanderthals and had babies together. That is why most people outside Africa still carry a tiny bit of Neanderthal DNA, about 1 or 2 out of every 100 parts.
Time moves on!

11 From hunters to farmers

About 12,000 to 9,000 years ago

For almost all of our history, people were hunters and gatherers. They moved from place to place to find food. Then, about 12,000 years ago, the last Ice Age ended. In the Middle East, people started to plant seeds like wheat and barley. They kept goats and sheep. Now they could stay in one place, so they built the first villages.

Göbekli Tepe in Turkey. People built these giant stone circles about 11,500 years ago. That is older than the pyramids! (Picture: Middle East Minorities, CC BY 4.0)

Einkorn wheat, one of the very first crops that farmers grew. (Picture: Roger Culos, CC BY-SA 3.0)

Çatalhöyük, a big village in Turkey from about 9,000 years ago. There were no streets, so people went into their houses through a hole in the roof, using a ladder! (Picture: Murat Özsoy, CC BY-SA 4.0)

Time moves on!

12 Cities, writing and kingdoms

About 6,000 to 4,500 years ago

Farms made lots of food, so villages grew into cities. People needed to remember who owned what. So, about 5,000 years ago, people in Mesopotamia (today's Iraq) invented writing! Soon there were great cities in many places: by the Nile in Egypt, and by the Indus river, not far from Bangladesh.

Some of the first writing in the world! The little marks on this clay tablet from Mesopotamia keep a record of barley. It is about 5,000 years old. (Picture: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, CC0)

Mohenjo-daro, a city of the Indus Valley, about 4,500 years old. It had neat streets, drains and a big bath. (Picture: Saqib Qayyum, CC BY-SA 3.0)

The pyramids of Giza in Egypt were built about 4,500 years ago. (Picture: Ricardo Liberato, CC BY-SA 2.0)

Time moves on!

13 Modern humans: you and me

The last few hundred years to today

In a very short time, humans learned amazing things. We made machines, electricity, medicine and computers. In 1969, people even walked on the Moon! Today, about 8.3 billion people live on Earth. However, our bodies are almost the same as those first Homo sapiens in Africa. If one of them were born today, they could go to school with you!

Earth at night, seen from space. Every bright spot is a city full of people. (Picture: NASA Earth Observatory, public domain)

A human footprint on the Moon, made in 1969. From footprints in ash at Laetoli to footprints on the Moon, what a journey! (Picture: NASA / Buzz Aldrin, public domain)

You are the newest page of this story! From one tiny cell, to fish, to tree-climbers, to walkers, to you. What will humans do next? Maybe you will help decide!

? Big mysteries still to solve

Scientists know a lot, but some questions are still open:

• Which old fossil is our real great-grandparent, and which ones are only cousins?
• Why did the Neanderthals disappear?
• Why did our brains grow so big?

Maybe you will dig up the next big fossil one day!
